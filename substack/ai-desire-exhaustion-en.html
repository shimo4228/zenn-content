Every morning I open my task list, and there is less to ask AI to do.

Loop engineering — building the repetitive parts of your work and life into loops that AI runs on its own — is the reigning thesis in AI coding circles right now.

In my case: article drafts, daily research, code review, a publishing pipeline. Each one I hand over means one fewer thing on my plate the next morning.

By the end of that shrinking, I could no longer think of anything new to ask.

Maybe the phase where individuals run out of things they want to do has already arrived. It has for me, at least.

The mechanism is sound — so why does nobody say it

Think about it, and this is the natural endpoint.

The list of things I want to do is finite, and AI burns through that list far faster than I can refill it. If replenishment can’t keep up with consumption, sooner or later you see the bottom.

Loop engineering is, in essence, a machine that eats your own demand. The better it runs, the less there is to ask.

A shrinking list of requests is, therefore, the surest evidence that your automation is working.

And yet you almost never hear anyone say this out loud.

Search for it, and you find the opposite story

I went looking for evidence, planning to write this up. What I found pointed the other way.

Report after report from workplaces says AI increases work. One survey even found that the heaviest users log the longest overtime.

Polished-looking AI output with nothing inside got its own name: workslop — work that offloads the burden of verification onto whoever receives it.

(Sources are at the end. Read the numbers with their dates attached.)

And on the discourse side, Japanese tech circles keep saying the core asset of the AI era is obsession — the passion you couldn’t quit if you tried. Competence is now something AI can produce, the argument goes, so what remains valuable is the heat only an individual can supply.

Far from running dry, work is multiplying and desire is being crowned the next scarce resource. Was my hunch simply wrong?

The new work and the new desires: are they real?

I didn’t think so. The key is what, exactly, increased.

Start with the organizational side.

Hours spent verifying AI output, learning tools, deciphering colleagues’ generated artifacts. None of this is work anyone was asking for before AI arrived.

It is processing for the sake of processing, generated by the processing power flooding in. Management scholars have started calling this “efficient inefficiency.”

Now the individual side.

Someone rebuilt Minecraft from scratch with leftover tokens. Someone spun up a clone of a famous web service over a weekend.

Look closely: this is reinventing the wheel. We gained almost unlimited capacity to build, and what gets built is what already exists.

Why do we keep rebuilding wheels? Perhaps because there is nothing we want to make, and no passion or obsession generating it.

Real desire points at things that don’t yet exist. Borrowing a ready-made blueprint means the goal is not to make something but to use something up.

If you pay a flat monthly fee for AI, feeling the leftover quota as waste is natural psychology. But the moment you start inventing “things you want to do” to soothe that feeling, the order has quietly reversed.

Rebuilding a wheel to learn or to play is one of the oldest genuine pleasures there is. The suspicious case is when interest dies the instant the thing is finished, and you move on to filling the next quota.

Stopgap work in organizations; wheel-reinvention at home. Different mechanisms, one common trait: neither begins with anyone’s “I want to.”

Which is why those reports are no proof that the well of desire never runs dry.

Even the stopgap work is being eaten by AI

There is also the matter of dates. The surveys showing that work increased all stop in 2025.

Contrast that with the individual side, where wheel-reinvention keeps flowing past this very moment as a stream of “built it with the newest model” posts.

A year in AI is long, and there is no guarantee the organizational record still reflects the present.

In my own environment, the shift has already happened: verification itself has begun migrating to AI.

Models from different lineages review each other’s output. Volumes of mechanical checks no human could sustain run continuously on their own.

The phase where a human verifies line by line, at least where I work, is nearly over.

The friction of the adoption era was real. But even that friction is now being eaten by AI.

Once even verification — that stopgap job — has been digested, what remains is the original question: an unburdened mass of processing power, and whether there is truly demand to match it.

Organizations are still inside the friction. But if my observation is right, when the friction dissolves, they will arrive where the individuals who finished their loops arrived first.

The place where there is nothing left to ask AI, and only processing power to spare.

Obsession was no way out either

Back to the claim that obsession is the core of the AI era. I don’t reject it.

My point is that if it is true, the exhaustion comes all the sooner.

Capacity to build is now available in surplus. “What do I want to build” is not.

Obsession gets called valuable because it sits on the scarce side. And if you feed the scarce thing as fuel into the abundant engine, the fuel runs out first.

This year a memoir drifted across Hacker News: eight years of a cherished idea, built in three months with AI. An enviable story.

But after eight years of stock is consumed in three months, where do the next eight years come from?

Here I should say something about myself.

Nothing I build is of a scale or nature that could be monetized. Almost all of it is small personal projects running on intrinsic motivation alone.

The obsession discourse, when it names the kind of person who will survive the AI era, is naming exactly this kind of person.

And I ran dry.

The thing you can’t quit even when told to is not something you can order in. Finishing a build does sometimes bring the next idea along, but not at a pace that keeps up with the spin of surplus processing power.

Obsession is slow-replenishing inventory. And inventory, however deep, eventually touches bottom.

What’s more, even if the inventory never runs out, that is no escape.

I have an obsession project of the can’t-quit kind. When I finished building its repetitive parts into AI loops, what happened was not consumption.

Once an idea becomes a pipeline, it simply flows.

The obsession is still alive and running. There is just almost nowhere left in it that needs my hands.

It didn’t run out. It left my hands.

What loop engineering eats is not only your list of wants. Even the continuation of an obsession that was supposed to keep going gets automated away.

There is one more standard prescription: leave implementation to AI and climb up a level, to judgment and strategy.

I don’t deny that value remains a level up. In that same obsession project, the one thing I have kept in my own hands is the judgment of what to add and where to grow it.

But that has become a once-a-week review. The level above turned out to be narrower than I expected.

And what I actually do in that review, when I’m honest about it, is re-decide a single thing: what do I want this to be.

Design judgment used to be precious because months of implementation cost were stacked beneath it. In a world where implementation takes one night, the mountain that guaranteed the view keeps getting lower.

And even up there, the list of things you want to do is still finite. The exhaustion doesn’t disappear. It just gets carried one level up.

Outlier humans whose ideas show no sign of running out do exist — the Elon Musks.

But “be like Musk” is the same species of advice as “be two meters tall.”

After the well ran dry, one desire was still moving

Since 2025 the English-speaking world has been arguing that the bottleneck has moved to taste: what to try, what to choose.

In 2026 a broader version appeared — AI doesn’t end scarcity, it relocates it.

But taste is a story about standing in front of options already laid out. I could barely find anything written about the layer beneath it: how much capacity to want remains.

The closest approach came from Fernando Borretti, an engineer and writer (May 2026).

Most people, he observed, have no real need they want AI to solve. Borrowing a phrase from Andy Matuschak, the tools-for-thought researcher, he called it the absence of a “serious context of use.”

From there I had to check for myself. After the asks ran out, was any desire in me still moving?

There was. Exactly one.

Thinking about why it ran out. The question of this exhaustion itself.

When desire loses its objects, it doesn’t seem to vanish. In me, at least, the desire with nowhere to go folded back and took the exhaustion itself as its object.

The proof is this essay. The last “thing to ask AI” left to a person who ran out of things to ask AI was writing about the running-out.

It sounds ironic. But as a mechanism, it’s natural.

If the last desire has the shape of a question, its natural destination is to be written.

This question was posed in 1930

This exhaustion has an old name, too.

In his 1930 essay “Economic Possibilities for our Grandchildren,” Keynes predicted that a century later, fifteen hours of work a week would be plenty.

And the real problem, he wrote, would begin there.

How does a human freed from pressing economic cares use that freedom? It would be humanity’s first “permanent problem.”

In the same essay Keynes wrote something meaner: for the ordinary person with no special talents, filling abundant free time would be a fearful problem.

The leisured classes of his day, already freed from economic necessity, he called humanity’s “advance guard.” Most of them, he added, seemed to have failed disastrously.

Eleven years after Keynes, Erich Fromm’s Escape from Freedom sketched a similar human habit from another angle: handed freedom, people flee into ready-made molds rather than endure the blank.

A landscape of wheels being rebuilt with leftover tokens looks like the modern edition.

The stock punchline about Keynes’s essay is that the fifteen-hour week never came.

But personal automation may be delivering, ahead of schedule, just the question at the prophecy’s center. Because the stock of things you want to ask AI runs out before you are ever freed from economic necessity.

And what lands in front of you then is exactly Keynes’s question. Freed from processing, what do I want?

This is probably a frontier

So what do the people who reached this question first have to say?

First-person testimony of “I ran out of things to ask AI” is still almost nowhere to be found, in Japanese or in English.

What you find instead: confessions of fatigue from delegating too much, and graveyards of projects built and abandoned. The words stop one step short of the exhaustion.

The silence isn’t hard to explain. Neither the people selling models nor the people selling productivity advice have any incentive to tell you your desires are about to run out.

As self-disclosure, the confession is a heavy one. “I have nothing left to ask AI” can sound like “I never had that much I wanted to do.”

Most people are probably also just mid-first-lap. But a finite list consumed faster than it refills is unlikely to be a condition unique to me.

So I expect people to arrive here in order, as they finish their laps.

I spent this half-year of fast-moving AI coding on the early side of the curve. I think I’ve seen where it leads.

I didn’t stop by hitting the ceiling of my capability. The opposite happened: capability overtook desire.

So I don’t think this place is a dead end. It’s a frontier many people will reach in turn, and it barely has words yet.

Land with no name on it is where concepts and values get coined next.

The vocabulary of AI adoption has so far been supply-side: what can it do, how much can you hand over.

The next phase’s vocabulary will be demand-side. And desire, too, leaves your hands, one pipeline-able part at a time.

What stays in your hands, to the end, is the question that can’t be built into a loop.

So, what do you want?

It’s starting to sound like a Zen koan, but I don’t mean it as metaphor. In a world where implementation cost has collapsed and processing power sits idle, this is the one question AI cannot answer for you.

I don’t have my answer yet. All I can say is that thinking about this question is the last desire I have left.

The one thing that has become clear is that the place to look for the answer is not inside the AI.
