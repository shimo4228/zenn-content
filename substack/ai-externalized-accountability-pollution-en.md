> **Context:** A companion essay extending the accountability-distribution framework of [Agent Attribution Practice (AAP)](https://github.com/shimo4228/agent-attribution-practice) into the layer of social consequence — how externalized accountability, when it can't be named, converges violently on the most visible individual. Published on Substack. For the underlying accountability argument, see AAP and the Zenn "AI Governance" series.

> Not a question of capability, but of where accountability goes. Externalized accountability doesn't disappear. The question is where it heads.

---

I keep thinking, lately, that AI can't really be monetized. Expertise, creation, research, code — AI is steadily thinning out the scarcity that used to justify charging for these things. What you could once fence off and sell, you can no longer fence off. You could even say the companies trying to profit from AI are burning down their own footing.

But it isn't revenue I'm interested in here. Because revenue isn't the only thing that can no longer be fenced off. Something else is leaking out, more quietly. Accountability — the ability to point, after the fact, at whose judgment it was.

## Benefit is internalized, harm is externalized

What came to mind here was pollution.

The structure of industrial pollution is always the same. A company gains a benefit from a new technology or a new industry. That benefit is enclosed inside the private firm. Meanwhile the costs — harm, health, the environment, future generations — are pushed outside the company, out into society. Benefit internalized, harm externalized. That asymmetry is what pollution is, at its core.

Look at AI agents the same way and the structure is much the same. The benefits — productivity, speed, compressed labor costs — go to the vendors and the adopting firms. Meanwhile the wrong decisions, the unexplainable behavior, the eroded jobs, the cost of recovery pile up on the side of users and society. And there's one more kind of externalization that pollution never had: the externalization of unexplainability itself.

Of course, it isn't exactly the same as pollution. The harm at Minamata — Japan's 1950s industrial mercury-poisoning disaster — was visible damage to bodies and lives. Much of the harm from AI agents is invisible and diffuse: eroded employment, disadvantages no one can quite account for. The timescale of cause and effect differs too. But it's precisely this "invisible and diffuse" difference that bites later.

## The missing effluent record

I've written before that an AI agent's black box has two layers. One is internalized in the model weights, and that one can't be read right now. The other is the layer humans built around it — the prompts, the rules, the tool definitions, the agent loop, the safety gates — and that one is readable in principle. Readable, yet kept hidden because it's a competitive edge. Not invisible technically — just not shown.

Translate that into the language of pollution and the second layer is the effluent record. What was discharged, when, how much. At Minamata, too, that was what got questioned most, afterward. What was in the factory's wastewater? Do the operating records survive? Without the records, the victims can't prove causation. The burden of proof piles up on the victims' side alone.

When something goes wrong with an AI agent, society asks the same thing. Which configuration of the agent was running? Which inputs reached it? Who approved that configuration? Without this minimum record — what I've previously called the minimum disclosure set, the smallest set needed to trace things after an incident — what the AI's "effluent" actually was can never be known.

## Accountability has two destinations

Here a fork appears. Externalized accountability doesn't simply vanish. But it has two destinations.

When accountability is **nameable** — when the harm can be attributed to a specific actor's specific act, and an institution can take that up — the anger flows into institutions. Litigation, compensation, regulation. It takes time, but it rarely turns into violence.

When accountability is **un-nameable** — when the harm is diffuse, when whose judgment it was, or even what happened, can't be traced — the anger doesn't disappear. It converges, violently, on the most visible individual.

Whether something is nameable is decided in two steps. First: is there a record of what happened? Second: is there a place in some institution for the actor who must bear the harm? The effluent record is the first step. And an AI agent's two-layer black box is missing even that first step — the record of what happened. So let's look first at what happens on the un-nameable side.

## When it can't be named — violence converges on a face

A community facing a crisis whose cause can't be pinned down often behaves strangely. In place of the real cause of the harm — diffuse, hard to grasp — it concentrates its violence on a single, substitutable victim. Once that victim is cast out, order seems, for a moment, to be restored, and the victim is mythologized afterward. This is the structure René Girard called the scapegoat mechanism. The history of blaming plague on Jews and foreigners is explained by it; the attribution of disaster and failed harvests in the witch trials reads as the same structure.

This is not an abstract tale from the past.

In December 2024, Brian Thompson, the CEO of the major American health insurer UnitedHealthcare, was shot dead on a Manhattan street. The shell casings had "delay," "deny," "depose" written on them in marker — delay payment, deny the claim, contest it — words said to be the insurance industry's standard tactics on claims. What's striking is what happened next. A fair amount of sympathy gathered around the suspect; he was spoken of as something like a folk hero.

Why did so much violence — and sympathy for that violence — gather on a single CEO? Not because he decided, alone, to deny claims. Because, within the vast and diffuse system of insurance, he was one of the few faces by which the locus of responsibility could be identified. The records of denied claims exist. But there is no institutional path by which a single denial can be attributed to someone's hardship or death. Even with the records, accountability can't be named. So the anger converges, not on the system, but on a single face. This looks like the scapegoat mechanism itself.

And here is where AI enters.

Before dawn on April 10, 2026, a 20-year-old man threw a Molotov cocktail at Sam Altman's home, then struck the glass doors of OpenAI's headquarters with a chair and was arrested after threatening arson. The man was carrying an anti-AI document that listed the names of executives at AI companies and wrote of the risk of human extinction. What strikes me is that, at this point, AI agents have barely been put into real-world use. No one killed by an AI misdiagnosis, no one mown down by AI at the wheel — none of it is confirmed yet. The picture of actual harm is almost nonexistent.

And yet a Molotov cocktail is thrown at a CEO's home. That the violence comes while the harm is still thin is because this isn't individual retaliation — it's anger with no address. When anger has no address, it doesn't disappear; it structurally goes looking for the most visible target. The model's interior can't be read. The scaffolding is a trade secret. There's no standard for incident records. There's no one to name. Insurance at least still had its records of denials; AI doesn't even have that. If anything, the un-nameability runs deeper. So the direction the anger heads narrows — not toward the abstract system, but toward a single face. The same structure as Thompson, run ahead of any actual harm, in AI.

Elish called the engineering version of this the moral crumple zone: in an accident, instead of the structure, it's the human nearest the loop who gets crushed. The scapegoat mechanism reads as its anthropological version. And in AI, the "nearest human" being crushed has leapt past the on-site operator all the way up to the company's face.

## When it can be named — anger flows to institutions

So what happens on the side where accountability can be named? Come back to pollution and, in fact, the opposite happens.

Chisso, the company behind Minamata disease, was condemned as the responsible firm. But no Molotov cocktail was thrown at the president's home. The anger flowed to the courts. Why? Because Chisso was a nameable responsible party, confirmed, and the effluent — the factory wastewater — left a record. Because accountability was attributable, it could pass through the institutional channels of litigation, compensation, and regulation.

And Chisso wasn't destroyed right away. It was kept alive as the entity that pays compensation — because letting it go bankrupt would have stopped the payments to victims. The national and prefectural governments got involved, and a structure was built to "keep the responsible company alive and make it pay." Compensation and lawsuits ran for decades; into the 2020s, litigation over uncertified victims still hasn't ended.

The same mechanism worked in the other pollution cases. In Itai-itai disease and in Yokkaichi asthma alike, residents named the companies and won in court — the first residents' victory among Japan's four major pollution lawsuits — and in the end the polluter-pays principle — that the cost of pollution is borne by the polluter — was codified into law.

This is what happens on the nameable side. The company isn't destroyed right away; it's kept alive precisely to pay, and it carries the litigation, the compensation, the regulation, and the social stigma across generations. Heavy — but this is not violence. It's institutional recovery.

Set the two side by side and the fork is clear. Toward the un-nameable Thompson and Altman, violence went. Toward the nameable Chisso, lawsuits went. The difference isn't whether the harm was evil. Isn't it simply whether accountability could be named?

## The condition under which "fail fast" could be a virtue

Pollution-era companies had their ethos too. Growth first, deal with the harm later. Build it, ship it, fix it on the run. The structure isn't so different from what Silicon Valley calls "fail fast."

But "fail fast" could stay a virtue only because, until now, the products they handled were things you could live without. Social media, SaaS, search — if they failed, you canceled, there was a backlash, you fixed it in the next version. There was harm, but it was mostly handled within the grammar of "product improvement."

AI agents go beyond that range. They become judgment machines, and then execution machines. They begin to handle hiring, credit, medical support, the routing of administrative procedures, and in some cases the support of military decisions. Once you're here, "just fail and learn" no longer seems to hold. Because the failure touches not the quality of a UX, but someone's life, rights, body, directly.

And in fact, what's happening right now heads in that direction. In the spring of 2026, OpenAI released a system called Symphony. The trigger was a bottleneck: a single engineer can comfortably watch only three to five coding-agent sessions at once, and beyond that the human's own context-switching can't keep up. Human attention, in other words, is the bottleneck. Symphony makes a task-management board the control plane, assigns one agent to every open task, and runs them continuously until done. The human no longer steers each step. They only review the results that come out. That same spring, Anthropic and Google too released managed agents that run autonomously for hours at a stretch. The mainstream of design is tilting toward moving the human from "steersman inside the loop" to "inspector of batched output."

There's no lie in this direction itself. Human attention really is scarce. But when you move the human from steersman to inspector, something thins out along with it. Approval, audit, records, separation of authority — the things that look like friction were also the devices that made harm nameable. Strip them away as "the human bottleneck," and what comes off isn't only delay. The attribution of which judgment belonged to whom dissolves into the batched output. "Fail fast" doesn't work in the direction of leaving records. It may be a choice that keeps AI on the un-nameable side — the scapegoat side.

## The ones who needed the leash first

Having come this far, my own position looks a little unexpected.

I've kept writing: distribute accountability, not capability. Leave a minimum record at incident time. If you're going to use autonomous judgment in the operation phase, name the responsible party. At a glance, these look like rules that are cold to AI — an obstacle to its proponents.

But lay the two regimes over each other and it looks like the reverse. This isn't a framework for stopping AI. It's a framework for moving AI to the "nameable" side. And isn't that also the condition under which the people who want to put AI into society don't get killed by society?

If accountability is institutionally distributed, you can separate things out: this was the behavior of this configuration, running under this approval, and this part was a human's judgment. At the very least, you can get out of the state where all the accountability concentrates on one face — the scapegoat state. It's the same as how the separation of powers, which looks like a device for binding the powerful, was also a device for keeping the powerful from being killed as tyrants — because it dispersed the face. Regicide was the oldest scapegoat mechanism, pinning the "face" of a diffuse injustice onto one person. To divide power, to record it, to keep it revocable, was also a device for protecting that face. The leash, too: it looks like a tool for binding the dog, but it protected both the dog and the owner. And the ones who needed the leash first were, probably, the people with the biggest dogs.

So the ones who first, and most urgently, need a framework like this aren't the AI skeptics, are they? The skeptics can just say "stop." But the proponents have to show how — without stopping — to make accountability nameable. Just as the polluter-pays principle was first needed, ironically, by the very industries that were producing the pollution.

## In closing — not "what could it do"

Of course, this doesn't mean it's solved. Who discloses the records, when, in what form, under whose audit authority? I wrote "the responsible operator," but the social anger probably won't stop there; it climbs to the top. Even after moving to the nameable side, the chance remains that the first face to be named is the one at the top. That part isn't worked out yet.

But one thing, at least, is clear. An un-nameable system draws violence onto a single face before any harm appears. A nameable system lets anger flow into institutions after the harm appears. The road from the former to the latter isn't to raise capability. It's to leave behind which configuration, which inputs, whose approval it ran under.

What gets asked, then, isn't "what could it do." It's "who can explain what happened." To put AI into society was never about distributing capability — it was about building the channels of accountability, wasn't it? When that becomes a widely shared question, I don't yet know.
